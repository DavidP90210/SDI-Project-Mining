WHAT IS THE PROBLEM WE ARE TRYING TO SOLVE?

Our engineering challenge is to develop a compact laboratory-scale flotation system that can separate valuable minerals from unwanted material while providing control over operating conditions.

Laboratory mineral processing can involve separate equipment for material preparation, grinding, flotation, and sample collection. Our project explores how these operations can be coordinated into an integrated platform that supports an accessible experimental workflow.

IMPORTANT TO KNOW

Froth flotation uses differences in particle surface properties to separate minerals. Under suitable conditions, water-repelling particles attach to air bubbles and rise into a froth layer for collection.

Slurry

A mixture of water and solid mineral particles.

Gangue

Unwanted material mixed with the valuable minerals.

Hydrophobic

A surface that repels water and can attach to air bubbles under suitable flotation conditions.

Concentrate

The collected product enriched in the desired mineral.

Tailings

The material remaining after separation; it can still contain unrecovered valuable minerals.

Agitation and aeration

An impeller circulates the slurry while an air supply introduces bubbles. Both affect the separation process.

The Flotation Process

  1. Prepare and load the mineral slurry.
  2. Agitate the slurry to keep particles suspended.
  3. Introduce air so suitable particles can attach to bubbles.
  4. Collect the mineral-rich froth and discharge the remaining slurry separately.

WHY IS THIS PROBLEM IMPORTANT?

Laboratory testing helps researchers compare operating conditions and understand mineral-separation behavior before considering larger systems.

Integration and Accessibility

Coordinating processing and collection stages could simplify laboratory work and sample handling.

Repeatability and Experimental Control

Adjustable operating conditions are intended to support repeated experiments and meaningful comparisons.

Intended Users

The system is intended for mineral-processing research and testing laboratories, academic researchers, and students.

These are design goals. Improvements in recovery, repeatability, cost, or efficiency have not yet been demonstrated in the materials presented here.

OUR PROPOSED SOLUTION

Our preliminary concept combines a flotation cell, variable-speed impeller, regulated air supply, froth collection, and tailings discharge. The broader integration goal includes material preparation, grinding, instrumentation, and sample collection.

Proposed physical and operating features for the laboratory-scale flotation cell. Component selection and integration remain subject to design review.

Cell Geometry

Bench-scale design with a near 1:1 aspect ratio, cylindrical or cubic.

Agitation

Bottom-mounted impeller with variable motor-speed control.

Air Injection

Forced-air system with a compressor, sealed tubing, and an adjustable flow valve.

Instrumentation

Pressure transducers, thermocouples, and flowmeters considered for slurry and air monitoring.

Slurry Handling

Spill tray and accessible drain for cleaner operation and maintenance.

Accessibility

Layout designed for easy adjustment, observation, and laboratory use.

FUTURE WORK

Planned next steps will be refined as the design develops.

  1. Review the cell geometry, impeller arrangement, air delivery, and collection design.
  2. Complete engineering calculations, component selection, and detailed CAD.
  3. Define interfaces with grinding, material preparation, and instrumentation.
  4. Fabricate and assemble the prototype with appropriate controls and safeguards.
  5. Develop a test procedure and evaluate mixing, airflow control, collection, and repeatability.
  6. Document results and use them to guide design improvements.
